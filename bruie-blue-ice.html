📅 2025-12-04 📍 Coordinate: -79.77, -83.26

Antarctica. The frozen continent. Home for these three weeks. And utterly devoid of the explosives we needed to make our investigations easier.

For almost ten years we’ve been working with Caltech on distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) technology, which uses a laser to sense changes in the strain of an optical fiber. If the fiber is well coupled to the surrounding surface, seismic motion can be inferred from the strain. And not only can this be done in one spot along the fiber, but it can be done thousands of times, creating a dense seismic sensor array. We can deploy on existing telecom fibers, such as those that deliver your internet, or we can deploy our own fiber. Using this tech, we’ve been able to map the inner structure of the Kilauea volcano, forecast future eruptions in Iceland (giving the populace about a 27 min heads up of fissures opening), and better map ongoing activity. In Pasadena, following the fires, this method was used to better map mud flows.

Now, in Antarctica, we’ll create a 3D movie of the inside of an ice sheet as it meets the ocean. The sheet moves up and down based on a tidal cycle and the glacier that feeds it slowly advances into the sea. Beneath the glacier, where the ice sheet meets the sea bottom, is the grounding line. At this spot, fresh water melt from the glacier mixes with salt water and creates flows that - at large scale (and Antarctic ice sheets are VERY large) - can drastically affect global ocean circulation.

We’re now ensconced in a field camp 40 miles from another soul (and only a few at that), living in tents with power and comms via solar panels and Starlink. The food is spectacular due to a camp chef, but necessary as we’re working around the clock to deploy sensors and run instruments. This is the fanciest field camp I’ve ever had - and it’s in the middle of blowing snow, clear, eternally sunlit skies, and temperatures well below zero.

It’s 3am and the sun is shining. We arrived here a few days ago via twin-otter aircraft, huddled amongst safety gear and double-sleeping bags rated for -80 deg. The team is in good spirits (though tired) and I’m contemplating building a snow tiki-bar to celebrate our soon-completion of all of the instrument deployments.
