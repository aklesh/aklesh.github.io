📅 2026-05-13 📍 Coordinate: 71.0365, -156.8129

Last fall, our Buoyant Rover for Under Ice Exploration (BRUIE) slipped into the water and drove under the ice of Sukok Lake some 30 miles south of Utqiavik (Barrow), Alaska. For over 15 years, our team has been visiting this lake and others in the surrounding region and using it as a lens to study the growth of microbial life that either produces or consumes methane. These organisms might be similar to what we might discover on Jupiter’s moon Europa - or Titan - or worlds beyond. It’s an early first step.

In October, deployment was different than usual. BRUIE was to stay under the ice for months, operated remotely, with recovery in the spring. At only a few meters deep, the lake would likely freeze to the bottom, encapsulating the rover and the various other sensors and cameras we had placed in the region.

For ten weeks, we operated remotely. BRUIE faithfully awoke each day, speaking over soundwaves on acoustic modems to basestations connected via Starlink antennas to operators located around the world. During last fall’s travels, I commanded our remote underwater explorer from as far away as Saudi Arabia and Antarctica. BRUIE served as an extension of our team, providing observations when we couldn’t be there, allowing humans to reach out and “touch” hostile environments.

Several weeks in, one of our Starlink connected basestations went offline. We could still ping it through the water via an acoustic modem, but couldn’t connect.

Last Thursday, we found out why.

This past week the team returned to Sukok to recover BRUIE and our other instruments. Over 6 days, we dug, drilled, chipped, melted, and sawed blocks of ice, trying to reach the encapsulated components. In windchills approaching -20, I ate squashed PB&J sandwiches and crawled under solar panels repairing circuit boards. We used a blowtorch to clean off and clear ice to peer beneath the surface. I spent a lot of time with chainsaws and rock hammers as we excavated a pit into the ice.

Oh, and we tried to hunt down pieces of our Starlink.

As best we can tell, a herd of caribou came by and used the Starlink on the southern base station as a scratching post. It’s horns likely got stuck, it got scared, and it jumped away, ripping off the cabling and internal circuit board, leaving the unit dangling in the wind.

Meanwhile, BRUIE was safely exploring under the ice.

This recovery trip was a mixed success. We never found BRUIE (he’ll pop up floating this summer), but we did recover underwater cameras, instruments, new samples, and other equipment.

In addition, we re-installed a basestation on shore with a camera to watch the ice breakup, and an under-ice camera to watch the re-start of a methane seep over the summer months. It’s remote operations once more - exploring an alien world, stretching out to expand humanity’s reach. It just happens to be on our own planet.

Along the way, we met and worked with some fantastic folks. Friends at WHOI had seen one of their instruments damaged in the region, so Joel and I were able to help them out by splicing some optical fibers. A new partnership arose when University of Alaska Fairbanks colleagues volunteered to search for BRUIE using ground penetrating radar. Like us, no luck, but there were many great conversations and strategies to use in the future.

In the science world, it’s always a collaboration. Something always goes wrong in the field (I’m looking at you, Mr. Caribou!), but it’s the people - and often their generously donated time - that makes it come together.

BRUIE will return.
